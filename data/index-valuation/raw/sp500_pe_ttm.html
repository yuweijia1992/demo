What is the Price / Earnings Ratio?

The price-to-earnings ratio is the price you pay for a security, divided by that security's earnings over some time. For the S&P 500, the Price to Earnings ratio is the weighted average earnings over all component stocks – in the same proportion as the index's market capitalization.

The traditional P/E ratio is measured over one year of earnings – the trailing twelve months. Notably, sometimes the Price to Earnings ratio is quoted for the Next Twelve Months (NTM), or even over the inflation-adjusted previous ten-year average (the Cyclically Adjusted PE, or CAPE).

Can the Price to Earnings ratio be used for market valuation?

Yes and no. Price to Earnings is a crude tool that can give you a snapshot of how fairly valued a stream of income is at the time, but it's a weak indicator.

As mentioned above, attempts to factor in longer history or future growth are potential ways to improve the indicator. Additionally, there are other ways to view profitability or the company's income streams – free cash flow, operating earnings, earnings before interest and taxes (EBIT), and earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization (EBITDA) are alternative views or profitability.

Also, viewing earnings across a market capitalized index like the S&P 500 introduces a bit of Simpson's Paradox. Different industries are valued based on various metrics and have different structural margins and earnings. For example, technology companies tend to have higher earnings margins and PE ratios than consumer staples. When one industry makes up a higher proportion of the index, the value may look amazing or poor on the surface... while different component industries have wildly different value.

Finally, earnings are vulnerable to both the business cycle and accounting and tax law changes. As noted by William Hester at Hussman Funds back in 2007, earnings tend to snap back after a recession, and companies tend to overstate losses during a recession's trough. Additionally, GAAP – generally accepted accounting principles – change over time and materially affect earnings and losses – see how stock-based compensation's accounting changed over time.

S&P 500 Price to Earnings Ratio Sources and Methodology

The monthly S&P earnings and index averages are sourced from Robert Shiller's compilation of index statistics. Shiller has data back to 1871, even though the S&P 500 in its current form started in 1957 (with a smaller index existing to 1926.) Data before 1926 comes from the Cowles datasets.

The recession indicators (the grey shading in the S&P PE graph) are from the National Bureau of Economic Research's "peak-to-trough" recession measure. Note that they may be revised in the future.

The index price is the monthly average closing price. Earnings is the trailing twelve-month earnings for the index, as compiled by Shiller. Where there is no earnings data, the tool extends the latest available figure (recent quarters are derived from S&P's published ratios and are approximate).

One last warning: data will be revised without warning. When earnings data is back-filled, the ratio will move materially.
